mcpt-lab

A backtesting platform designed and built by David DeConti

Almost any trading strategy looks profitable once it has been tuned on past prices, even on prices that are pure noise. mcpt-lab asks a harder question: does the strategy beat the same tuning process run on hundreds of shuffled markets?

Where does the real market land?

The idea

Shuffle the order, keep everything else

A permutation takes the real price bars and rearranges them. The shuffled market has the same moves: the same volatility, the same fat tails, the same start and end price. The only thing destroyed is the order. Trends, reversals and every other pattern in time are gone. If a strategy's edge depends on those patterns, it should disappear on shuffled data. If the strategy does just as well on shuffled data, its backtest came from tuning, not from the market.

The method, after neurotrader888

Four steps. Stop at the first failure.

  1. Step 1

    In-sample excellence

    Tune the strategy on training data, with trading costs. Is the best version any good?

  2. Step 2

    In-sample permutation test

    Run the same tuning on 1,000 shuffled copies. Does the real market give more than noise does?

  3. Step 3

    Walk-forward

    Re-tune on a rolling window, then trade the next block of data the strategy has never seen.

  4. Step 4

    Walk-forward permutation test

    Repeat the walk-forward on shuffled futures. Is the out-of-sample result better than luck?

I added three stricter tests. A sign-flip test keeps volatility in place and randomizes only direction. A post-publication test freezes a published strategy's parameters and runs it only on data from after it was published. A label-shuffle test checks whether a machine-learning filter beats one trained on scrambled answers.

What the platform found

One edge survived, and it is fading

Fit to noise

Donchian breakout

Tuning reached a profit factor of 1.11 on S&P 500 futures. The median shuffled market reached 1.12. On data it had not seen, it lost money.

Real, small, fading

Volatility Hawkes

Passed all four steps and the sign-flip test. On 2023–2026 data, created after the strategy was published, it still beat shuffled markets (p = 0.026). But it was weaker in each phase.

Died after publication

Trendline + ML filter

In 2020–22 the filter beat 99.5% of filters trained on scrambled labels. After publication it could no longer be told apart from them (p = 0.195).

Follow-up study · preregistered

Ten famous strategies, before and after they were published

Academic finance has published hundreds of "anomalies", patterns that seemed to beat the market. I took ten of the most famous and ran each with its published rule, with no tuning, on 100 years of US stock data (1926–2026). Each one is measured three ways: in the authors' own sample, after the paper came out, and after realistic trading costs and slippage.

30%of the in-sample return was left after publication, pooled across 8 strategies (p ≈ 3×10⁻⁹)
9 / 9anomaly strategies earned less after publication than in their authors' sample
0kept a statistically reliable profit after publication once trading costs were paid

Why calendar effects lasted: nobody could afford to trade them

the effect, before costs   cost of trading it   shaded = the effect beat its cost. 10-year rolling averages, % per month; the scale compresses large values so negatives and zero stay visible.
  • Discovered but untradable. Before 1975, trading the whole market cost about 1% each way. Net of that, the Monday effect lost 6.8% a month and turn-of-the-month lost 1.1% a month in their authors' own samples.
  • Cheap to trade, but smaller. After S&P futures (1982) and decimal prices (2001), the costs fell. By then the effects had shrunk: turn-of-the-month earns +0.15% a month after costs (p = 0.087), which is not reliable.
  • High turnover gets eaten. Short-term reversal loses money after costs even in its own sample. Momentum's post-publication return (0.39%/mo) is below its measured cost (0.65%/mo).
  • One claim held. Faber's 10-month moving-average rule promised lower risk, not higher return. After publication its worst drawdown was −18%, against −50% for buy-and-hold, mostly thanks to 2008.

Design after McLean & Pontiff (2016). Data: Kenneth R. French Data Library. Costs: Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) and Jones (2002). Limits: "after publication" is also "later in time", the cost estimates are approximate, and famous strategies may overstate decay. This is a replication of a known result, extended to 2026, not a new discovery.

Testing the tester

Checked on fake markets where the answer is known

Before trusting it on real data, I ran the test on 90 synthetic markets. Some were pure noise, with realistic fat tails and volatility clustering. Others had a trend deliberately planted in them.

What I built

The platform

  • A Python library anyone can install. Write a strategy as one small class, and the platform handles costs, tuning, permutations, logging and plots.
  • A ledger that records every test automatically, so a lucky p-value can always be judged against how many ideas were tried.
  • Documentation, runnable examples and a one-minute quickstart that needs no downloads.

Designed and built by David DeConti. Code: github.com/dnd8639-byte/mcpt-lab. The permutation method, the four-step process and the two BTC strategies come from neurotrader888's MIT-licensed work: mcpt, VolatilityHawkes, TrendlineBreakoutMetaLabel.

A research and education tool, not investment advice. Its main lesson is how rarely a backtest survives an honest test.
