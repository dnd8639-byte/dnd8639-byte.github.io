Preregistered futures research

Thirteen tests of well-known strategy families: trend-following, momentum, carry, spread mean-reversion and front-running the commodity-index roll. Each test was written down before any data was run. None passed.

Method

Write the rules first, then run the test

Before each round I wrote a preregistration: the strategy, its fixed parameters, the data, the costs and the pass/fail rule, dated before any result existed. Every change made after a result was seen is recorded as a disclosed amendment, and both versions are reported.

  • A strategy passed only if it cleared three gates: a bootstrap confidence interval above zero, a Deflated Sharpe Ratio above 0.95 (which raises the bar for every test already tried), and a positive result on the most recent years.
  • Every test counts. The trial count only goes up. After 11–13 tests, a strategy needed a net Sharpe ratio of about 0.68 to pass.
  • The tester was tested first. Each round began with a harness on synthetic data: a planted edge had to be found, pure noise had to show nothing, and a deliberate look-ahead "canary" had to be caught.

Findings

What the failures taught

  • The historical momentum effect was real but small, and it has faded. On 98 years of industry data, shuffling the order of returns destroyed it (p = 0.009) and ordinary market exposures did not explain it. Since 2016 it has been flat to negative. The professional benchmark (AQR's trend factor) fell from a Sharpe of about 1.5 to 0.14 over the same period.
  • Implementations were right; the edges were weak. My trend strategy correlated +0.82 with AQR's published trend factor. The signals pointed the right way, but 16 years of clean data cannot tell a Sharpe of 0.2–0.35 apart from luck after 11 tests.
  • Bad data and small bugs manufacture profits. Three examples, each measured on data with no edge at all:
    • sizing positions with the same month's volatility produced a fake Sharpe of about +0.2;
    • using monthly-average prices instead of month-end prices produced a fake Sharpe of about +0.9 (and would have "passed" every gate);
    • fitting pairs-trading hedge ratios with hindsight produced about +0.5 on unrelated random walks.
  • Commodity data from free sources is badly distorted. Yahoo's continuous futures overstated natural-gas returns by about 24% a year, because roll losses were hidden in the splice. I rebuilt the futures series contract by contract from exchange data.

Notes

Limits and data

  • Clean futures data covers only 2010–2026, so small effects are hard to detect. A power analysis showed that even a real effect of the historical size would pass the strictest gate only 14–21% of the time.
  • The exchange data is licensed and cannot be redistributed. Long-history data comes from the Kenneth R. French Data Library and public sources.
  • This work led to mcpt-lab, a reusable platform for the same kind of honest testing.
